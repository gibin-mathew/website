Trust Issues: a platformer that lies to you

Most platformers are honest. The floor is floor, the exit is the exit. I wanted one where none of that holds, and the game is quietly on the level's side instead of yours.

The idea

Reach the door. The door disagrees.

That is the whole pitch. You get a platformer that looks completely standard for about four seconds, and then it starts working against you. The exit is not where it was. Something you stood on last time will not hold you this time. The game is not broken, it is doing exactly what it was built to do, and that gap between the two is the joke.

Difficulty can be changed at any point, including mid-run, because the alternative is people closing the tab. There is a charged jump if you hold the button after landing, a shop, cosmetic hats you unlock by surviving Endless mode, coins from playing and gems from optional ads or finding the secret keys. No real money, ever. I did not want to build the kind of game that asks you for your card.

How it got built

I had the idea and the design calls. Claude wrote most of the code with me, across a few weeks of evenings.

That is worth saying plainly rather than hiding, because it is the same way I work in the day job. At Edvoy my teams use models to write and repair scrapers, draft validations and do the first pass on data nobody has time to read by hand, and a person still signs off on anything that matters. The difference here is that nothing mattered. No reviewers, no stakeholders, no cost of being wrong. Turns out that is a good way to find out what the method is actually capable of when you take the guardrails off, which is a thing I now know in my hands rather than in theory.

The parts that took the longest were the parts I did not expect. Touch controls that do not feel like mud. A jump that forgives you slightly more than it should. Keeping the whole thing one self-contained build with no backend to pay for. Game feel is not a thing you can specify, you can only keep playing it until it stops annoying you.

Why it is on this site

Because I think it is good and I made it. That is the honest answer.

It is not a case study and I am not going to pretend there is a deeper lesson in it about trust in automated systems, however neatly that would have tied up. If you came here looking for the work I would actually put my name on in a hiring conversation, that is over in projects, with the numbers attached.
